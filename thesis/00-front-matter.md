**A Thesis**

Author: Sergio
Date: August 2026
Status: Working draft v0.3 — with measured results from a research prototype

## Abstract

Modern systems of record are pulled in two directions at once. The write path demands an authoritative history: strictly serializable, durable, immutable, auditable and retained. The read path demands the opposite economics — an open-ended family of derived views whose access is expected to be skewed, with most of them cold most of the time. That skew is a hypothesis this thesis measures rather than a premise it assumes: no published measurement of read concentration in operational banking was found (§1.2), and the one workload description available to it, the author's, reports mild activity skew. In banking, that family is not merely large but *unbounded by regulation*: a bank must serve a customer's available balance net of authorization holds, the balance as of any historical date on both the booking and value-date axes, positions rolled up by product, currency, desk, legal entity and geography, month-to-date statements, and real-time features for fraud and anti-money-laundering scoring — and supervisory principles require it to answer *ad hoc* aggregations it has not seen before. Today these demands are served by gluing an OLTP database, a change-data-capture pipeline, a cache fleet and a warehouse together, and the seams are where correctness dies. The failure mode has a regulatory name: when two derived views of one ledger — one counting settled transactions, one netting unsettled holds — disagree at the moment a decision is made, the result is the authorize-positive-settle-negative pattern that consumer-protection guidance addresses directly.

This thesis proposes a theory of how both demands can be served coherently from one semantic foundation: an authoritative, append-only, hash-chained, strictly serializable base that is never partial, and a family of derived read models that are *partially stateful* — materializing only what is read, evicting the rest, and reconstructing evicted state on demand through upqueries anchored to a requested epoch. The general case is domain-free — an authoritative base relation with committed writes — and the double-entry ledger, monetary types and bitemporal semantics are a domain layer over a fully general relational core. The central object of study is a new abstraction, the **reconstructible epoch-anchored view (REV)**: a versioned, partially materialized derived state over an immutable, epoch-ordered base whose epochs define a global visibility timeline, carrying a declared contract for consistency, freshness, materialization mode, memory budget, lineage and retention.

Seven results are stated around it: four proved, one partly proved, one partly measured and one specified, as the register in §1.9.1 records. A **versioned partial-state algebra**, compatible with the DBSP incremental-computation framework, with a lattice giving formal meaning to a key's absence and an **epoch-anchored reconstruction theorem** whose corollary is that eviction followed by reconstruction can never create or destroy money, proved for the linear keyed fragment the runtime executes — and which dissolves, by construction, the five anomalies partial-state dataflow must otherwise exclude by protocol. The **Eviction–Consistency Frontier Theorem**, a PACELC-style boundary result: strictness forces a miss at the top rung to wait for the base, and the cost model then says where strict-serializable partial materialization stops being cheaper than full materialization — parameterized by skew, budget, write rate and reconstruction latency — and when that crossover exists at all. A **complexity theory of consistency under partial materialization**, proving that the price of each rung of an epoch-based consistency ladder — from bounded staleness to ledger-consistent strict serializability — is shaped by the workload, not by the length of history. A **consistency-effect calculus** with a soundness theorem guaranteeing that a well-typed program conserves value per currency, cannot mismatch currencies and cannot overdraw without authorization — while stating precisely what such a theorem cannot buy, since non-negativity is provably not coordination-free. An **adaptive materialization calculus** choosing per view and key range among absent, demand, full, spilled and tiered state under each view's contract, with an explicit account of which classical caching results survive reconstruction latency and which do not. It is specified and not built, and no competitive ratio is claimed. A **generality result** replacing the unprovable slogan "replaces SQL" with three checkable statements: relational completeness, fixpoint completeness over the epoch-ordered base — where the ledger's own total order supplies the ordering hypothesis the theorem requires — and a semantics-preserving translation of a stated SQL fragment — a restructuring forced by the discovery that no authoritative, current SQL conformance suite exists, the last official one having targeted SQL-92 and been withdrawn in 1997. And a **Bounded Reconstruction Theorem**, produced by experiment rather than derivation, showing that reconstruction cost is bounded by a per-key checkpoint interval (measured: flat at ≈ 8.5 base rows across a 64× increase in history, against a predicted C/2 + 1 = 9) and is *not* bounded without that mechanism.

The theory is tested by two instruments: **Niles**, a state-aware language whose novel constructs carry the theory's vocabulary — bases and ledgers, money with per-currency scale, value dates and bitemporality, idempotency windows, materialization boundaries, per-view consistency, lineage, retention and confidentiality — over a fully general relational core; and **Nilestream**, a from-scratch streaming DBMS pairing a ledger-grade write path with a partial-state read-model runtime over a typed intermediate representation that is the stable contract between language and engine, speaking PostgreSQL's wire protocol. Neither is the product. The product language is PostgreSQL's SQL and PL/pgSQL with a checked dialect, of which Niles is the specification and reference checker, because a measured comparison found the dialect refuses every defect class Niles refuses but one; the product engine is PostgreSQL, extended in SQL and PL/pgSQL first.

Three of the measurements **contradicted claims made in earlier drafts of this thesis**, and are reported as contradictions rather than quietly removed: that partiality's advantage grows with skew (it does not — full materialization's own footprint shrinks faster, so the memory ratio moves *against* partiality as skew rises); that per-key anchor indices make reconstruction cost history-independent (they reduce the constant by two orders of magnitude and change nothing asymptotically); and that hot-account contention was measurable on this instrument (it is not — the prototype is single-threaded, and the flat result is a non-result). ## Reading Guide

Chapters 1–4 are the scientific core: the problem, the inheritance, the formal framework, and the six contributions. Chapters 5–9 are the instrument and its test: methodology, architecture and language, implementation status, the phased program with its kill criteria, and the evaluation design. Chapters 10–13 situate, discuss and conclude.

Appendix A retells the whole thesis in metaphors for non-technical readers, including what has not been done. Appendix J adjudicates the competing design positions this thesis has held, deciding each with its evidence and recording the losing position; Appendix K documents the prototype and gives the commands that regenerate every measured number. Appendices B–I are normative references: the Niles language, the compilation model, the engine and IR, the self-hosting compiler, the runnable conservation-of-money reference model, the build kit, the generality and SQL-completeness dossier, and the adaptive materialization optimizer.

A reader with one hour should read Chapter 1, Section 4.1, and Section 9.6. A reader deciding whether to trust the system should read Chapter 3 and Appendix F. A reader assessing the novelty claims should read Sections 4.8 and 10.11. A reader who wants to write Niles should read Chapter 6 and Appendix B.
