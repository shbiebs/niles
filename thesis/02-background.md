# 2. Background

This chapter assembles the inherited material the thesis builds on. Nothing here is new; everything here is load-bearing. Chapter 10 returns to the same families to state differences; this chapter states what they give us.

## 2.1 The Double-Entry Ledger

Double-entry bookkeeping, codified by Pacioli in 1494 and unchanged in essence since, records every economic event as a set of postings that sum to zero: every debit has equal and opposite credits. Three properties make it the oldest integrity constraint in continuous production use. It is *local* — balance is checked per transaction, not by a global scan. It is *compositional* — the sum of balanced transactions is balanced, so the global invariant follows by induction from per-transaction checks. And it is *append-only by convention* — an erroneous entry is corrected by a reversing entry, never by erasure, which is what gives the paper ledger its audit value.

Contemporary implementations restate all three. Uber's payments platform describes its zero-sum rule as ensuring that "no money can ever be created or destroyed," with money orders immutable and adjustments captured as additional money orders. Square's Books requires that "all transactions (which we call 'journal entries') must balance to 0, so each cent lost is matched with a cent gained," and enforces append-only at the schema level: "there are no update statements for the tables presented on the diagram, only inserts." Modern Treasury documents the same discipline and a design-space disagreement worth noting: in its model "a ledger transaction is mutable while pending and immutable once posted," whereas TigerBeetle keeps even the pending transfer immutable and resolves it by appending a new transfer. This thesis takes the stricter position, for the reason given in Section 3.19: if a hold is itself an immutable ledger fact, then available balance is a derived view like any other, and no part of the system needs an update path.

In database terms a ledger is a relation of postings with the constraint that, for each transaction and each currency, amounts sum to zero. Balances are not stored facts but *derived views*: the balance of an account is the sum of its postings up to a moment. Nilestream takes that literally (Section 6.7).

## 2.2 The General Case: Authoritative Base Relations and Committed Writes

The ledger is a special case of something plainer, and the thesis's core is built on the plain thing. A **base relation** is authoritative: its rows are facts the system is the source of truth for. A **committed write** is an atomic, durable, ordered addition to a base relation, admitted only if it passes the constraints declared for that base. In the general core there is no money, no debit and no credit — only a base, an append, an order, and a commit rule.

The specialization to a ledger is then a matter of what the commit rule says and what the columns mean: the commit rule enforces a per-group sum-zero constraint; one column carries an idempotency key; two carry the time axes; one carries a currency-indexed amount. Everything else — epochs, hashing, derived views, partial materialization, reconstruction, consistency contracts — is defined at the general level and inherited. This is the sense in which "the general case for a ledger or posting is the general case of an authoritative base table and committed write," and it is what makes the banking layer a library rather than a fork (Section 6.6).

Two consequences are worth stating early. First, every theorem about reconstruction, cost and consistency is proved over base relations and applies unchanged to non-financial domains — the inventory-conservation domain of Section 9.11 exercises exactly this. Second, the invariants that *are* specific to money enter through the type system and the commit rule, not through the engine's core, which is why the trusted computing base does not grow when a new domain library is added.

## 2.3 Incremental View Maintenance and Dataflow

A materialized view V = Q(D) can be kept current under changes ΔD either by recomputation, V′ = Q(D + ΔD), or by incremental maintenance, V′ = V + ΔQ(D, ΔD), where ΔQ is a *delta query* that is cheap when ΔD is small. The literature spans four decades: counting algorithms for duplicates, delta rules for select-project-join, higher-order maintenance where the delta queries are themselves incrementalized (DBToaster), and treatments of recursion. The engineering embodiments are timely and differential dataflow and the systems above them.

Two inherited lessons matter. First, incrementality is an algebraic property: if data lives in a group so that deltas can be negative, every linear operator is its own delta form and non-linear operators have systematic delta forms. Second, consistency in dataflow is a *frontier* notion. Naiad's progress tracking formalizes this with pointstamps — "Each event has a timestamp and a location … and we refer to these as a pointstamp" — and a *could-result-in* relation whose minimal elements form the frontier of active pointstamps [Murray et al., SOSP '13]. Both lessons are absorbed in Chapter 3, with one important distinction: a Naiad frontier is a *progress* frontier, a lower bound on what may still arrive, whereas this thesis's visibility frontier is a *commitment* frontier over a totally ordered base — the difference is that nothing can arrive "before" a sealed epoch, so the frontier is a fact rather than an estimate.

Differential dataflow contributes one further mechanism the design reuses: *shared arrangements*, indexed multiversioned collections of immutable batches shared across operators and queries, which "substitute for per-instance operator state in the dataflow, and should appear to an individual operator as if it was a private copy of its state" [McSherry et al., PVLDB '20]. Sharing immutable indexed state across readers is exactly what an immutable base makes safe.

## 2.4 Partially Stateful Dataflow and Upqueries

Noria introduced *partial state* into dataflow: an operator's state — and the final materialized view — may contain holes. A read that hits a hole issues an **upquery**, a request that travels *up* the dataflow toward the base tables for retransmission of the data needed to fill the hole; responses flow back down as ordinary positive updates. Eviction re-creates holes. Partially stateful operators "start out fully evicted and are gradually and lazily populated by upqueries," and "eviction notices flow forward along the update data-flow path" so that operators can drop updates addressed to evicted entries [Gjengset et al., OSDI '18].

Gjengset's dissertation states the three invariants such a system must maintain: (I) "All reads reflect each base table change at most once"; (II) a read observing the effects of a base-table change also observes all effects of earlier changes to that table along the same dataflow path; and (III) if an operator encounters missing state while processing a record, downstream state reflecting that record must be evicted. It also names the five anomalies partial state must exclude — **double application**, **skipped deltas**, **upquery races**, **lost deltas**, and **upquery deadlock** — plus the join-specific hazards of dependent upqueries and incongruent joins. Upquery plans are derived by *key provenance*, tracing a view's parameter column back to a column in upstream state.

Noria's consistency is stated exactly: "Noria does ensure that if writes quiesce, all external views eventually hold results that are the same as if the queries had been executed directly against the base table data" — eventual consistency, with randomized eviction, and with transactions and stronger consistency listed as future work. Its published resource figures for the Lobsters deployment are instructive about the *shape* of the win: full state was 789 MB, roughly 8× the 137 MB base tables, while with partial state the non-partial residue was 73 MB, about 9% of total state.

The negative space is precise. Noria's mechanism is right and its guarantees are insufficient for money. What that mechanism needs — an immutable, totally ordered base; versioned holes; upqueries anchored to a requested epoch; per-view contracts — is what makes the REV, and is why the five anomalies become inexpressible rather than defended: they are all consequences of reconstructing against a *moving* base.

## 2.5 Consistency Semantics for Banking

The consistency vocabulary is standard and is assembled here for precision. *Linearizability* is Herlihy and Wing's correctness condition for concurrent objects [TOPLAS 1990]. *Strict serializability* is commonly stated as serializability plus real-time order — but the attribution deserves care: Papadimitriou's SSR is defined over the history's own operation order, not over external real time [JACM 1979], and the real-time formulation is the Herlihy–Wing composition. The thesis states the distinction explicitly (Section 3.8) because the epoch-ordered visibility timeline sits exactly at that seam.

*Session guarantees* — read-your-writes, monotonic reads, monotonic writes, writes-follow-reads — are due to Terry et al. and are stated as predicates over which writes are included in a server's database state at a time [PDIS 1994]. That predicate form is the template this thesis adopts. So is the decomposition style of Viotti and Vukolić's survey of more than fifty non-transactional consistency notions, which expresses each model as a conjunction of independently varying predicates (a single-order condition, a real-time condition, a return-value condition) [ACM CSUR 2016].

*Bounded staleness* has a shipping precedent worth copying: Azure Cosmos DB parameterizes it by "'K' versions (that is, 'updates') of an item or by 'T' time intervals, whichever is reached first." The dual parameterization, and the whichever-comes-first rule, are adopted for the ℓ₀ rung in Section 3.7.

What is achievable at all is bounded by the HAT results: read committed, monotonic atomic view, item and predicate cut isolation, and the monotonic session guarantees are highly available; read-your-writes and causal consistency are *sticky* available; snapshot isolation, repeatable read, one-copy serializability and linearizability are unavailable [Bailis et al., PVLDB 2013]. The ladder of Chapter 3 is therefore not a free menu: its lower rungs are the HAT-achievable set and its top rung is provably in the unavailable set, which is the honest framing for a per-view contract.

Banking practice does not require every read to be strictly serializable — a statement may lawfully be seconds stale — but the *authorization* decision must reflect every committed posting, and the write path's order is legally meaningful. The design consequence, argued in Section 3.7, is asymmetry: one maximal-consistency write path, many per-view read contracts.

## 2.6 The DBSP / Z-set Substrate

DBSP is the formal substrate the algebra of Chapter 3 is compatible with. A stream is a function from discrete time to values; a **Z-set** over A is a function with finite support from A to ℤ, so deletion is insertion with weight −1 and every change is addition. Four constructs generate the universe: lifting of scalar functions, the delay z⁻¹, integration I(s)[t] = Σ_{i≤t} s[i], and differentiation D(s) = s − z⁻¹(s).

The theorems the thesis leans on are stated in the source: integration and differentiation are inverses (Theorem 2.20); incrementalization is compositional via the chain rule (Q₁ ∘ Q₂)^Δ = Q₁^Δ ∘ Q₂^Δ, with a cycle rule extending it through recursion (Proposition 3.2); linear time-invariant operators are their own incremental form, Q^Δ = Q (Theorem 3.3); and bilinear operators — joins — have the systematic delta form of Theorem 3.4. Complexity is stated too: linear operators incrementalize to O(|ΔDB|) time with zero space; joins to O(|DB| × |ΔDB|), better than re-evaluation by a factor of O(|DB|/|ΔDB|); and `distinct` incrementalizes to O(|ΔDB|) time with O(|DB|) space [Budiu et al., PVLDB '23]. Coverage includes the relational core, grouping and aggregation, nested relations, and recursion with a correctness theorem tying the circuit's output to Datalog semantics.

The thesis leans on DBSP for three things: F2's duality is stated as a theorem about I and D; the partial-state algebra is defined so that every REV's query is a DBSP circuit, inheriting incrementalization for the *maintenance* path while the thesis adds the *partiality* dimension DBSP does not model; and the typed IR is a DBSP-style circuit language, so the implementation interprets the formal objects rather than merely resembling them.

## 2.7 The Relational Model, SQL, Generality, and Expressive Power

Codd's relational model earned its dominance through data independence, a small closed algebra, and declarativity that admits optimization. This thesis abandons none of that. What it abandons is SQL as the surface, and the case must be made in terms of expressive power rather than taste.

The relevant results are old and precise. Codd defined relational completeness relative to his calculus ALPHA and proved the algebra relationally complete [RJ987, 1972]; the modern statement — algebra equals safe, domain-independent calculus — is developed in Abiteboul, Hull and Vianu. Relational calculus cannot express transitive closure, a limitation Aho and Ullman identified and answered by proposing a least-fixpoint operator [POPL 1979]. Adding that operator is decisive: Immerman's theorem states that relational calculus plus least fixpoint, over structures with a total ordering relation, expresses exactly the queries computable in polynomial time [Immerman 1982/1986; independently Vardi 1982]. The ordering hypothesis is essential to the theorem — and an epoch-ordered ledger supplies exactly such a total order as part of its data model, which is a genuine structural argument for the design rather than a coincidence. Beyond PTIME, Chandra and Harel's computable queries define what a *complete* query language means at all [JCSS 1980].

The standard itself has been moving toward the thesis's concerns for fifteen years. SQL:2011 added application-time period tables and system-versioned tables, where "historical rows cannot be modified by users," with `AS OF SYSTEM TIME` queries and a closed-open period model — bitemporality, standardized [Kulkarni & Michels, SIGMOD Record 2012]. SQL:2016 added JSON functions (but no JSON *type*) and row pattern recognition. SQL:2023 added a JSON data type and, as a new Part 16, property graph queries (SQL/PGQ). And in 2024 ISO published GQL (ISO/IEC 39075), the first new ISO database language since SQL — developed by the same working group, with a full create-read-update-delete surface over property graphs rather than SQL/PGQ's read-only view.

Two conclusions follow for this thesis. First, the accretion pattern is real: each capability the modern workload needs arrives as an optional feature bolted to a fifty-year-old grammar, and none of them are typed in the sense that matters here — none makes staleness, conservation, or retention checkable. Second, and more usefully, GQL is an existence proof that the standards community will define a *new language* rather than another extension when the model genuinely differs, and that "an embedded read-only sublanguage" and "a standalone full language" are recognized as different artifacts. The language-creation gate of Section 6.10 is argued against exactly this backdrop — including against the strongest opposing position, "One SQL to Rule Them All," which argues that streaming needs only three additions to standard SQL: time-varying relations as the foundational abstraction, event-time semantics with watermarks, and a small set of keywords controlling materialization of results [Begoli et al., SIGMOD '19].

## 2.8 Unbounded Data and the Log

The log-centric school holds that the append-only ordered log is the primitive from which databases are derived. Its academic form is older than its industrial fame: CQL's semantics already treat a relation as "a mapping from 𝒯 to a finite but unbounded bag of tuples," with three operator classes connecting streams to relations — stream-to-relation (windows), relation-to-relation (ordinary SQL), and relation-to-stream (`Istream`, `Dstream`, `Rstream`, defined pointwise against the previous instant) [Arasu, Babu & Widom, VLDB J. 2006]. CQL's claim is a *reduction* — given those three classes you obtain a complete continuous-query semantics — not a duality theorem, and this thesis is careful to attribute accordingly.

The industrial statement is Kreps's log abstraction and the stream-table duality of the Kafka lineage, where a table is the materialization of a stream up to an offset and a changelog is the table's stream of successive updates [Sax et al., BIRTE '18]. The Dataflow model contributes the watermark vocabulary for reasoning about completeness of unbounded input [Akidau et al., PVLDB 2015]. The Lambda/Kappa debate contributes the negative lesson: maintaining two codepaths for one semantics is an integration tax a single incremental substrate removes.

What none of this supplies is the read-side economics — their derived state is total — or end-to-end guarantees across the write/read boundary. That is what the REV is for.

## 2.9 Workload Evidence: Skew, Working Sets, and Their Limits

The thesis's economic premise is that access to derived state is skewed. This premise must be inherited from measurement, and the measurements disagree.

Twitter's analysis of 153 in-memory cache clusters — 54 analyzed in detail, ~700 billion requests, 80 TB of traces — reports that 80% of workloads fit Zipf with R² > 0.8 and more than half with R² > 0.9, with fitted α "in the range from 1 to 2.5," and a sharp split between read-heavy (median α ≈ 1.4) and write-heavy (median α ≈ 0.9) workloads. It also reports that more than 35% of the clusters studied were write-heavy (>30% writes) and more than 20% had write ratios above 50% [Yang, Yue & Rashmi, OSDI '20]. Facebook's earlier memcached study reports power-law-like popularity — "50% of ETC's keys occur in only 1% of all requests" — but publishes no fitted α [Atikoglu et al., SIGMETRICS '12].

The counterweight is CacheLib, whose authors state that "prior models have been too optimistic about the cacheability of production workloads," reporting that of four Meta workloads only Lookaside is Zipfian with α near 1, while SocialGraph shows α ≈ 0.55 and CDN α ≈ 0.7, and noting that lower α means a much larger share of requests goes to the tail [Berg et al., OSDI '20].

This thesis treats the disagreement as a first-class design input rather than a nuisance: it is precisely why the materialization decision must be *measured and adaptive* (C5) rather than assumed, why the phase diagram is the headline deliverable, and why S1's protocol sweeps α down to 0.55 rather than stopping at 0.9.

## 2.10 Caching Theory, View Selection, and Adaptive Structures

The decision "materialize this or not" has a mature theory in three literatures the thesis draws on.

**Competitive paging.** Sleator and Tarjan established the resource-augmented bound for online paging — LRU's fault count is at most k/(k − h + 1) times the offline optimum's with a smaller cache, and no deterministic online algorithm does better — with Belady's MIN as the offline optimum [Sleator & Tarjan, CACM 1985; Belady, IBM Syst. J. 1966]. Randomization helps by a logarithmic factor: the marking algorithm is 2H_k-competitive and no randomized algorithm beats H_k [Fiat et al., J. Algorithms 1991]. Crucially for this setting, the *weighted* generalization exists: in file caching, where objects have heterogeneous sizes and retrieval costs, Young's Landlord algorithm attains the same k/(k − h + 1) form [Young, Algorithmica 2002], and heterogeneous retrieval cost is exactly what upqueries of differing depth impose.

**Rent-or-buy.** Whether to pay repeatedly (upquery on every miss) or once (materialize and maintain) is the ski-rental problem: the break-even strategy is 2-competitive, and randomization achieves a ratio approaching e/(e−1) ≈ 1.58, which is optimal [Karlin et al., Algorithmica 1994]. This is the skeleton of the per-key materialization decision in C5.

**Delayed hits.** The classical results assume a miss is resolved instantaneously. Upqueries are not. Atre et al. formalize the *delayed hit*: requests arriving while a fetch is in flight are neither hits nor full misses, and the decisive parameter is Z, the ratio of fetch time to mean inter-arrival time. Under this objective, "traditional caching strategies — even so called 'optimal' algorithms — can fail to minimize latency," and the authors demonstrate that Belady's MIN is not latency-optimal [Atre et al., SIGCOMM '20]. They also report a competitive lower bound of Ω(kZ) for deterministic online algorithms, attributed to parallel work — a result whose shape is directly relevant to this thesis's frontier, and which is cited here as attributed rather than as their own.

**View and index selection.** Choosing which views to materialize under a space budget is NP-complete by reduction from set cover, and the greedy algorithm achieves benefit at least (1 − 1/e) of optimal — but *under a cost model in which "the cost of answering Q is the number of rows present in the table for that query"* [Harinarayan, Rajaraman & Ullman, SIGMOD '96]. That linear cost assumption is exactly what partial materialization violates, since the cost of answering from a partially materialized view depends on the miss rate and the reconstruction depth, not on the view's size. The violation is a contribution hook, not an oversight: C5 must either restore a submodular structure or give up the greedy guarantee explicitly. Index selection is likewise hard [Comer, TODS 1978], and the practical lineage runs through AutoAdmin's cost-based enumeration with what-if indexes and its Greedy(m,k) search [Chaudhuri & Narasayya, VLDB '97] to the self-driving DBMS agenda [Pavlo et al., CIDR '17].

**Adaptive structures.** Database cracking states the thesis's own principle for physical layout: "Each query is interpreted not only as a request for a particular result set, but also as an advice to crack the physical database store into smaller pieces" [Idreos, Kersten & Manegold, CIDR '07]. Adaptive merging refines it, and the comparison is instructive: Graefe and Kuno report that merging is "practically complete after about 40 queries" where cracking needs on the order of 5,000, because of the difference between a fan-out of two or three and a merge fan-in in the hundreds [EDBT '10]. *How* demand-driven filling is done can move the break-even by orders of magnitude — a caution the optimizer design takes seriously. What none of these systems has is a consistency dimension: cracking reorganizes physical storage, not derived views, and asks no questions about visibility.

## 2.11 Coordination Avoidance and Contended Records

Two independent literatures agree on where coordination becomes unavoidable, and their agreement is the backbone of this thesis's frontier.

**Invariant confluence.** Bailis et al. define I-confluence — a set of transactions is I-confluent with respect to an invariant if, for all reachable states with a common ancestor, their merge is valid — and prove it necessary and sufficient for invariant-preserving coordination-free execution [PVLDB 2015]. Their catalogue is directly applicable: attribute equality and inequality are I-confluent under any operation; materialized-view update and secondary indexing are I-confluent; a `>` threshold with *decrement* is **not**. In this thesis's terms: **conservation of value is a sum-equality invariant and is coordination-free, while "balance must not go below zero" is provably not.** Contribution 1's corollary is defensible partly *because* conservation is I-confluent; Contribution 4's no-unauthorized-overdraft guarantee necessarily costs coordination, and Bailis is the citation that forbids wishing that away.

**Contended-record techniques.** O'Neil's escrow method maintains, per field, the lowest and highest values the field could take given which in-flight transactions commit or abort, and admits an increment or decrement without a lock exactly when the operation cannot violate any outstanding condition regardless of the outcome of concurrent transactions [O'Neil, TODS 1986]. Barbará and Garcia-Molina's demarcation protocol generalizes this to distributed sites by granting each a safe local range — "lines drawn in the sand" — adjustable by bilateral exchange [VLDB J. 1994]. Doppel's phase reconciliation splits contended records into per-core slices during a split phase and reconciles them, under four stated conditions, of which two matter here: splittable operations must commute with themselves, and must return nothing — so a *read* of a split record forces a return to the joined phase [Narula et al., OSDI '14]. Firestore's sharded counters make the same trade in the simplest possible form: writes scale linearly with shards, reads become O(shards), and no aggregate threshold can be enforced.

Three literatures, one boundary: blind conservation-preserving addition is cheap and splittable; a threshold on the aggregate is not. TigerBeetle's pending transfers are escrow made concrete as immutable ledger records, and its `credits_must_not_exceed_debits` flag is the non-I-confluent constraint given a first-class home. Section 3.19 builds on exactly this.

## 2.12 Provenance and Lineage

Full traceability of operations, changes and states is a requirement of the system and has an established formal home. Semiring provenance annotates tuples with elements of a commutative semiring, with positive relational algebra mapping union and projection to `+` and join to `·`. Its central factorization theorem states that for any positive query and *any* commutative semiring, the semantics factors through polynomial provenance — so ℕ[X], polynomials over tuple identifiers, is the most general provenance, and every other semantics (set, bag, probabilistic, incomplete-database) is recovered by a semiring homomorphism [Green, Karvounarakis & Tannen, PODS '07]. Why-, how-, and where-provenance are distinguished in the standard survey [Cheney, Chiticariu & Tan, 2009].

Two results are directly load-bearing. First, Z-sets are the (ℤ, +, ·) case, so the thesis's algebra is already a semiring image, and provenance-preservation under reconstruction can be obtained as a corollary of the factorization theorem rather than proved from scratch. Second — and this is prior art the thesis must engage rather than rediscover — ORCHESTRA used semiring provenance *specifically* to maintain derived state incrementally, choosing its provenance formalism for exactly that property, and reported that its provenance-guided deletion algorithm "is faster than a full recomputation even when deleting up to approximately 80% of the instance" [Green, Karvounarakis, Ives & Tannen, VLDB '07]. That is a break-even claim of the same *kind* as this thesis's frontier, published in 2007, and Section 4.3 positions against it explicitly.

For streaming and dataflow specifically, Ariadne instruments operators to annotate tuples with provenance during processing, supporting eager and lazy (replay-based) generation with compression [Glavic et al., DEBS '13], and Titian provides interactive-speed lineage for Spark at typically under 30% overhead [Interlandi et al., PVLDB 2015]. The overhead figure is the order-of-magnitude reference for S9.

## 2.13 Tamper Evidence and Verifiable Structures

Hash chaining and Merkle trees provide *evidence*, and the literature says so in terms the thesis adopts verbatim. RFC 6962 describes the append-only property as achieved through Merkle trees that "can be used to show that any particular version of the log is a superset of any particular previous version," split-view attempts as detectable "by comparing tree roots and consistency proofs," and states outright that "the logs do not themselves prevent misissue, but they ensure that interested parties … can detect such misissuance." QLDB's journal is hash-chained into blocks with digests described as "a unique signature of your data's entire change history as of a point in time," and O(log n) verification — with the scope stated precisely: what is proved is that a revision was not altered *between the time a digest was saved and the time verification runs*.

Trillian's *log-backed map* is the structural precedent for the whole architecture: "Any changes to the verifiable map are entered into a verifiable log, and only when present in the log are they applied to the verifiable map." Citing it strengthens rather than weakens the thesis's position, since the novelty claimed is the partial-materialization layer above that pattern, not the pattern. Finally, LambdaAuth shows that authenticated data structures can be made a *type-system* property — all well-typed programs compile to operations secure under collision resistance [Miller, Hicks, Katz & Shi, POPL '14] — which is the closest precedent for Contribution 4's move of making auditability statically checkable.

## 2.14 HTAP, Multi-Model, and the One-Size-Fits-All Debate

A general-purpose DBMS today is expected to serve transactional and analytical work together and to accommodate document, graph, time-series and search access patterns. The relevant history is a dialectic. Stonebraker and Çetintemel argued in 2005 that the one-size-fits-all RDBMS was obsolete and specialized engines would win [ICDE '05]. Kemper and Neumann answered with HyPer and a paper titled "One Size Fits all, Again!", arguing that main-memory hardware invalidated the premise, using `fork`-based virtual-memory snapshots so that OLAP sessions run on a consistent snapshot while OLTP proceeds [BIRTE 2010; ICDE '11]. The HTAP literature is the empirical continuation, and a recent survey organizes it by four storage architectures: primary row store with in-memory column store; distributed row store with column-store replica; primary row store with distributed in-memory column store; and primary column store with delta row store [Zhang et al., TKDE 2024].

The systems instantiate these differently. SAP HANA holds that "it is no longer reasonable to continue the classical distinction between transactional and analytical access patterns," combining row and column tables in one SQL statement. TiDB replicates from a row store (TiKV) to a columnar store (TiFlash) through a novel Raft *learner* role that neither votes nor counts toward quorum, with consistency enforced at read time [Huang et al., PVLDB 2020]. SingleStore's Universal Storage "internally makes use of both rowstore and columnstore formats, but end users need not be aware of this," with an in-memory skiplist rowstore for recent writes over an LSM columnstore. Oracle Database In-Memory keeps the same table in both formats transactionally consistently, arguing the column store pays for itself by replacing analytic indexes. F1 Lightning takes the opposite architectural stance — "HTAP as a service," deliberately not co-designed with the transactional engine.

For multi-model, the survey position is Lu and Holubová's [ACM CSUR 2019]; the concrete precedents this thesis reuses are PostgreSQL's `jsonb` (a decomposed binary representation that is "significantly faster to process, since no reparsing is needed" and supports indexing), Azure DocumentDB's schema-agnostic indexing that treats documents as trees of paths, Gorilla's time-series compression (delta-of-delta timestamps where "96% of all time stamps can be compressed to a single bit," XOR'd values where 51% compress to a single bit, 1.37 bytes per point overall) [Pelkonen et al., PVLDB 2015], and Lucene's immutable segments with tombstones and tiered merging.

This thesis's position within the dialectic is stated in Section 6.5: the *storage* debate is orthogonal to its claims — Nilestream's derived read models can be row-shaped, column-shaped, inverted, or time-partitioned as the optimizer chooses, because they are all REVs over the same base. What HTAP systems achieve by dual formats over a mutable table, Nilestream achieves by multiple derived views over an immutable base, which is the same idea with a stronger correctness story and a partial-materialization dimension the HTAP literature does not have.

## 2.15 The Regulatory Frame: Retention, Aggregation, Value Dates, Erasure

Four regulatory facts shape requirements, and each is stated here in its own terms because the thesis's design claims lean on them.

**Retention with an audit-trail alternative.** SEC Rule 17a-4 requires preservation of specified records for not less than six years (the first two "in an easily accessible place") and others for three, and — since amendments adopted 12 October 2022, effective 3 January 2023 — offers two ways to satisfy the electronic-records condition: preserve records "exclusively in a non-rewriteable, non-erasable format" (WORM), *or* "preserve a record … in a manner that maintains a complete time-stamped audit trail," including all modifications and deletions, the time of each action, the identity of the actor where applicable, and enough information to "permit the recreation of an original record if it is modified or deleted." That second alternative is, in regulatory language, precisely this thesis's architecture: a complete, attributed, time-stamped change log sufficient to reconstruct any prior state.

**Aggregation.** BCBS 239's fourteen principles include the four that constrain a read-model architecture directly: accuracy and integrity (aggregation "on a largely automated basis so as to minimise the probability of errors"), completeness (data available "by business line, legal entity, asset type, industry, region and other groupings"), timeliness — explicitly conditional on simultaneously meeting accuracy, completeness and adaptability — and adaptability, which requires meeting "a broad range of on-demand, ad hoc risk management reporting requests, including requests during stress/crisis situations" [BCBS d328, January 2013]. The 2023 progress report on the 31 assessed G-SIBs found that "significant work remain[s] at most banks to fully adopt the Principles" [BCBS d559].

**Value dates.** PSD2 Article 87 constrains the relationship between value dating and settlement: the credit value date for the payee "no later than the business day on which the amount … is credited to the payee's payment service provider's account," and the debit value date for the payer "no earlier than the time at which the amount … is debited." This is a legal constraint relating the valid-time axis to the transaction-time axis — the strongest available argument that bitemporality belongs in the type system rather than in application convention.

**Erasure versus immutability.** GDPR Article 17 sits in genuine tension with a never-forgetting base. The most authoritative published treatment is CNIL's, which states that "it is technically impossible to grant the request for erasure made by a data subject when data is registered on a blockchain," and offers the crypto-shredding mitigation — where data is stored as a commitment, keyed hash or ciphertext, deleting the verification elements or the secret key can "make the data practically inaccessible, and therefore move closer to the effects of data erasure." The hedge in that phrasing is deliberate and this thesis preserves it: crypto-shredding approaches the effect of erasure; CNIL does not say it satisfies Article 17. Section 11.1 accordingly lists legal-erasure-over-base-facts among the workloads where this design is the wrong choice.

## 2.16 Existing Research Behind the Thesis

Gathered so that Chapter 10 can concentrate on differences: provenance and lineage underlie the upquery's claim to recompute exactly a key's slice; MVCC underlies versioned reads; hash-chained and Merkle-authenticated structures underlie tamper-evidence; deterministic execution (Calvin, Aria) underlies epoch-batched commit; type-and-effect systems, linear/affine types, units-of-measure typing, refinement types and information-flow typing underlie the calculus; bitemporal database theory and SQL:2011 supply the two-calendar model; competitive analysis, view selection and adaptive indexing supply the optimizer's theory; coordination avoidance and escrow supply its limits; MLIR's dialect-and-progressive-lowering philosophy [Lattner et al., CGO 2021] and data-centric query compilation [Neumann, PVLDB 2011] supply the compiler architecture; WebAssembly and WASI supply the UDF sandbox — with the important caveat, taken up in Section 6.13 and Appendix C, that Wasm is *not* fully deterministic: the specification names NaN payloads, resource exhaustion, and host functions as implementation-dependent [Haas et al., PLDI '17]. The thesis's novelty claims are always relative to this inheritance.
