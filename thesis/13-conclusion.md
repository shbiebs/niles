# 13. Conclusion

This thesis began from a mismatch every system of record lives with. The write path wants an immutable, strictly ordered, never-forgetting history. The read path wants the economics of a cache, and wants them across an open-ended family of derived views — available balances net of holds, balances as of any historical date, positions rolled up by product, currency, desk, legal entity and geography, statements, fraud features — whose membership regulation itself declines to bound, since a bank must answer "a broad range of on-demand, ad hoc" aggregation requests. Industry serves the pair by gluing components together, and the seams are where correctness, auditability and money leak; the failure has a name in supervisory guidance, and it is what happens when two derived views of one ledger disagree at the moment a decision is made.

The claim of this thesis is that the mismatch dissolves under one abstraction studied rigorously: the **reconstructible epoch-anchored view** — versioned, partially materialized derived state over an immutable, epoch-ordered, hash-chained base, where the base in the general case is an authoritative relation with committed writes, and the double-entry ledger is that general case with a commit rule and typed columns.

Seven results were stated and proved around that object. The **versioned partial-state algebra and reconstruction theorem** established that eviction and reconstruction are exact at every anchor, with the corollary that memory pressure can never create or destroy money — and, as an engineering dividend, that the five anomalies partial-state dataflow must otherwise exclude by protocol become inexpressible once the base stops moving. The **Eviction–Consistency Frontier Theorem** bounded the approach honestly, proving that the cost a miss pays at the top rung is forced by the rung rather than by any implementation choice, and deriving from the cost model both where partial materialization stops being cheaper than full materialization and the condition under which that crossover exists at all. The **complexity theory of consistency under partial materialization** priced every rung of the ladder in workload shape alone, freeing the cost of correctness from the length of history. The **consistency-effect calculus** moved conservation, currency discipline and authorization from runtime policing into typing judgements, while declaring precisely what such a theorem cannot buy — non-negativity is provably not coordination-free, and no type system repeals that. The **adaptive materialization calculus** specified a runtime policy over a five-point mode lattice — specified, and not built — and reported as a negative result that the most-cited guarantee in the view-selection literature does not survive the move to partial state. And the **generality result** replaced an unprovable slogan about replacing SQL with three statements that can be checked — relational completeness, fixpoint completeness over the epoch-ordered base, and a semantics-preserving translation of a stated SQL fragment — with the ledger's own total order supplying, at no cost, the ordering hypothesis that the fixpoint theorem requires.

The theory was given an instrument precise enough to falsify it: **Niles**, a general-purpose, SQL-first, Rust-fallback language whose novel constructs carry the theory's vocabulary — bases and ledgers, money with per-currency scale, value dates and two time axes, idempotency windows, materialization modes, per-view consistency, lineage, retention and confidentiality — with banking as a library over a fully general relational core; and **Nilestream**, a streaming DBMS pairing a ledger-grade write path with a partial-state read-model runtime over a typed intermediate representation that is the stable contract between language and engine, wire-compatible with MySQL and PostgreSQL so that adoption can be incremental. Four foundational hypotheses — unboundedness, stream-relation duality, alignment, and the immutable-base-plus-derived-state shape — were formalized with their independent, dependent and control variables and their falsifiers; the duality was proved within an established operator algebra and sharpened with the information-asymmetry lemma that makes the stream, not the table, primitive.

What this thesis has, and earlier drafts did not, is measurements — of the mechanisms, not of a database. A research prototype implementing epoch-ordered commit, hash chaining, anchor indices, checkpoints, partial materialization over the absence lattice, anchored upqueries and three eviction policies was built and run under five fixed seeds, reporting machine-independent counted work. It corroborated what the theory predicted: across roughly 13,600 reconstructions under continuous eviction, no reconstructed value ever differed from an independent fold of the ledger; per-currency conservation held exactly on every run; the entire derived layer was wiped and rebuilt from the base with every balance matching; integration and differentiation round-tripped as canonical Z-sets at 1,000 consecutive epochs; and a measured phase boundary appeared where the theory said one should be.

It also refuted three things this thesis had asserted. Partiality's advantage does **not** grow with skew — full materialization's own footprint shrinks faster, so the memory ratio moves against partiality as access concentrates, and what actually governs the trade is the price of memory relative to reconstruction, at an interior optimum budget. Per-key anchor indices do **not** make reconstruction cost history-independent — they buy two orders of magnitude in constant factor and nothing asymptotically, because a hot key under skewed access keeps a roughly constant share of a growing traffic total. And the prototype **cannot** speak to hot-account contention at all, being single-threaded, so its flat result on that axis is an absence of experiment rather than an absence of problem.

The second refutation was the productive one. Testing why the claim failed identified the mechanism that makes it true — a per-key checkpoint, from which reconstruction folds only the suffix — and measuring that mechanism confirmed the bound its theory predicts: cost flat at about 8.5 base rows across a sixty-four-fold increase in history, against a predicted C/2 + 1 = 9. That is now the Bounded Reconstruction Theorem, the seventh contribution, and the only one that an experiment produced rather than confirmed. It also explains, in retrospect, why production ledgers maintain running balances instead of folding journals on demand: they are buying the same bound under a different name.

What remains unmeasured is stated as plainly. There is no durability, no concurrency, no distribution and no compiler in the prototype, so there is no throughput comparison against any database, no isolation-level result, and no language claim. Those cells are marked *to be measured*, with their protocols and their pre-registered predictions, and none is filled with an invented number. Two claims were withdrawn outright rather than weakened: that SQL-completeness could be established by passing a conformance suite, since no authoritative current suite exists and the last official one was withdrawn in 1997; and that a conservation-of-money suite is evidence of strict serializability, since a published analysis shows a system passing the former while violating the latter.

The contribution, finally, is the theory: a formal account of versioned, partially materialized state over an immutable ledger with epoch-based consistency semantics — what it guarantees, what it costs, how to decide, and where it ends. If the phase diagram holds, institutions gain a principled instrument for a choice they currently make blind. If it fails, the failure will be localized, attributable, and itself a result. Either way the system was never the point. The system is the experiment; the understanding is the deliverable.
